# The social value of spontaneity, exploration and planning

An interdisciplinary evidence map of a society's need for different ways of thinking and acting

**Evidence class:** external literature scoping synthesis. **Search completed:** 2026-10-31. **Research context:** DD-024; the review itself contains no new experiment or result claim.

This is the preserved interdisciplinary review that motivated the behavioral-diversity discovery campaign. It is a broad landscape search, not a preregistered systematic review. Every proposed new experiment must run its own focused novelty check against both this literature and existing repository studies.

## Executive findings

**Yes: several academic fields support important parts of the intuition that society benefits from people who explore, improvise, depart from convention, and change direction.** The strongest evidence concerns the functions those behaviors perform. It does not establish that people who plan their lives less constitute a single personality class, or that a particular proportion of such people maximizes societal wellbeing.

The closest established framework is **exploration versus exploitation**: discovering alternatives versus developing and using existing knowledge. A recent synthesis identifies **maintaining transient diversity** as a common mechanism behind better collective problem solving. Complementary cognition and diversity advantage theories go further, explicitly proposing benefits from different cognitive or personality profiles, but remain substantially theoretical. [1-5]

The best-supported reformulation is: **Under uncertainty, groups can benefit from varied approaches and exploratory activity when they can evaluate discoveries, share useful information, and turn promising ideas into reliable outcomes.** Planning, persistence, and flexibility can coexist within the same person. Whether stable personality specialization adds an advantage over people who switch strategies intelligently remains an open question.

This report maps 51 selected academic sources across personality and cognitive psychology, career research, management, entrepreneurship, economics, social psychology, sociology, cultural evolution, biology, network science, and philosophy of science. It distinguishes experiments, observational evidence, meta-analyses, and theoretical models, and ends with a concrete design for testing the proposed societal hypothesis.

## 1. Scope, method and how to interpret the evidence

This is an interdisciplinary scoping review, completed on 31 October 2026, rather than a preregistered systematic review. Searches used scholarly publisher pages, PubMed and PubMed Central, institutional repositories, author-hosted papers, and reference following. Search families included exploration-exploitation, personality and creativity, coping flexibility, goal adjustment, planned happenstance, effectuation, planning and performance, minority dissent, collective intelligence, social learning, strategic experimentation, and cognitive division of labor. Recent searches extended through 2026.

Priority went to original studies, formal models, and research syntheses. Accessible full texts were consulted where available; some paywalled studies could be checked only through their abstracts and publication records, so claims about those studies stay within what those records establish. This review does not claim exhaustive database coverage, provide a screening-flow count, or pool the studies into a new statistical estimate.

Three distinctions govern the conclusions: **individual benefit versus collective benefit; a behavioral strategy versus a stable personality; and a result inside a model versus evidence about actual societies.** A correlation between openness and a creativity test does not establish that a society benefits from more nonplanners. A simulation can demonstrate a possible mechanism without demonstrating that its agents accurately represent people.

| Proposition | Assessment from this landscape |
|---|---|
| Exploring alternatives and developing existing solutions both matter | Strong conceptual foundation; supported by multiple bounded experiments and organizational studies. |
| Some randomness or independence can improve collective outcomes | Direct experimental support in particular coordination and search tasks; substantial boundary conditions. |
| Openness and adaptive flexibility can help individuals | Meta-analytic support for modest associations with creative thinking, adjustment, and wellbeing. |
| A mix of spontaneous and planful personalities always improves teams | Mixed and insufficient evidence; some personality-composition results run against this claim. |
| Society has a known optimal ratio of planners to nonplanners | Not established by the literature reviewed. |
| Human personality diversity evolved to maximize social welfare | A proposed interpretation in some theories, not a demonstrated conclusion. |

## 2. Translate 'chaotic' into separable, testable characteristics

The everyday label combines several different characteristics. Openness concerns interest in unfamiliar ideas and experiences. Exploration is behavior that samples alternatives. Flexibility is adjustment to circumstances. Spontaneity concerns how an action begins. Low orderliness concerns a preference for less structure. Impulsivity concerns acting with insufficient forethought. These are not interchangeable constructs. Personality research also separates industriousness and orderliness within conscientiousness. [6-10]

A person can be highly exploratory and highly disciplined. A person who dislikes calendars can still keep commitments and work persistently. Conversely, 'going with the flow' can mean following everyone else's preferences, which may reduce rather than increase the diversity of a group's approaches. These are logical counterexamples to treating planning and novelty as opposites.

An informal two-axis map is therefore more useful than a single continuum. The categories below are illustrative, not a validated personality classification. The same individual can occupy different cells across domains and stages of a project.

|  | More advance structure | Less advance structure |
|---|---|---|
| More exploration | Systematic experimentation; a disciplined researcher testing unconventional ideas | Improvisational discovery; opportunistic experiments and changing directions |
| Less exploration | Reliable operation and refinement of established practices | Habitual drift or following prevailing trends without much independent search |

## 3. The central framework: exploration and exploitation

March's 1991 organizational-learning models are the closest foundational match. Exploration introduces possibilities; exploitation refines and uses what is already known. Exclusive exploration can leave useful ideas undeveloped, while exclusive exploitation can trap a system in an inferior approach. Because exploitation often pays sooner, learning processes can overfavor it. This is a model of organizational learning, not a study of life-planning personalities. [1]

Smaldino and colleagues' 2024 synthesis connects diverse mechanisms through one idea: preserve alternative solutions long enough to evaluate them before the group settles. Separate working groups, intermittent communication, different search methods, and even behavioral inertia can maintain this temporary diversity. Better eventual answers can come at the cost of slower agreement. The synthesis assumes conditions such as sufficiently shared goals and solutions that can be compared. [2]

The conceptual implication is broader than 'unpredictable people generate change.' A group can lose useful alternatives because everyone copies too quickly. It can also fail because alternatives never become actionable knowledge. Variation, evaluation, communication, and implementation all belong in the explanation. This four-part account is a synthesis of the literature, not an independently measured law of society.

## 4. Personality and creativity: openness matters; disorganization is a weaker explanation

A 2023 meta-analysis by Grajzel, Acar and Singer included 63 studies and 24,298 participants. Openness correlated with divergent thinking at r = .20; extraversion's association was smaller. This supports a modest relationship between exploratory interests and generating possibilities. Divergent thinking is an indicator of creative potential, not a direct measure of completed innovation or societal benefit. [6]

Feist's classic 1998 meta-analysis reported a more unconventional, sometimes less conscientious creative profile, but also greater drive and ambition. The mixture matters: creative accomplishment does not simply correspond to low effort or weak commitment. A later large meta-analysis by Stanek and Ones distinguished personality facets and found industriousness positively related to originality/creativity ability. Occupational selection, measurement choices, and different definitions of creativity help explain why these findings need not conflict. [7, 8]

Wilson and colleagues' 2014 decision experiment distinguishes **directed exploration**, which seeks informative options, from **random exploration**, which varies choices. Participants increased both when more future decisions remained. This shows that even variability can be deployed strategically. It does not prove random choice was optimal: the task's optimal model used no decision noise. [9]

Useful implication: an unconventional thinker might contribute through searching overlooked possibilities, combining distant ideas, or persisting with an unusual question. None of these requires unreliability.

## 5. Life planning, adaptability and wellbeing

Cheng and colleagues' coping-flexibility meta-analysis covered 58,946 participants and found an overall association with psychological adjustment of r = .23. Crucially, measures emphasizing a good fit between strategy and situation performed better than measures emphasizing simply changing strategies or possessing a broad repertoire. The benefit is associated with responsive adjustment, rather than variability alone. [10]

Riddell and colleagues' 2025/2026 review brought together 235 studies and 1,421 effect sizes on goal adjustment. Letting go of an unattainable goal, finding a new goal, and flexibly pursuing a goal have different associations with outcomes. Reengagement has a clearer connection to positive wellbeing than disengagement alone. The authors judged the accumulated evidence low to moderate because of cross-sectional designs, heterogeneity, and publication-bias concerns. [11]

Planning also has substantial support. Aeon and colleagues' 2021 meta-analysis of 158 studies associated time management with performance and wellbeing. Their definition includes adapting time as well as structuring and protecting it. The classic implementation-intention literature likewise finds that specifying when and how to act helps goal attainment. These studies address time use and goal execution, rather than choosing a detailed lifelong script. [12, 51]

Moffitt and colleagues followed approximately 1,000 children into adulthood and linked childhood self-control to later health, finances, and offending, with a supplementary sibling comparison. This is strong longitudinal evidence of predictive associations, not a randomized test. It reinforces the need to distinguish exploratory freedom from difficulty regulating behavior. [13]

The most defensible personal interpretation is to retain meaningful commitments while revising routes and sometimes destinations. Research on goal adjustment supports this more directly than either 'plan your entire life' or 'never make plans.'

## 6. Careers, serendipity, leisure and incubation

Mitchell, Levin and Krumboltz's **planned happenstance** framework explicitly treats unplanned events as potential career opportunities. People can actively create more opportunities through exploration and develop the capacity to use them. Kim and colleagues later followed 307 Korean college students during their transition to work: planned-happenstance skills preceded better life adjustment. That observational study supports a bundle of skills, not nonplanning by itself. [14, 15]

Pryor and Bright's **chaos theory of careers** is another close conceptual match. It emphasizes complex influences, chance events, and nonlinear change. It provides a vocabulary for why career trajectories resist detailed prediction; its foundational paper is theoretical and does not show that a chaotic personality produces better outcomes. [16]

There are narrower benefits to leaving life less scheduled. Across 13 studies, Tonietto and Malkoc found that precise scheduling could make leisure feel more like work and reduce enjoyment; rough scheduling without exact times removed the effect. Sio and Ormerod's meta-analysis also found benefits from setting a problem aside and returning later, with task and break conditions moderating the result. These findings concern particular activities and alternating modes, not a globally superior lifestyle. [17, 18]

## 7. Entrepreneurship and organizations: adaptation can work with planning

Sarasvathy's effectuation theory distinguishes starting with a specified outcome and choosing means from starting with available means and developing possible outcomes. Effectuation fits the active, opportunistic interpretation of 'going with the flow': build with what is available and respond to contingencies. The original paper offers a conceptual theory, not a causal performance estimate. [19]

Empirical research favors a qualified combination. Brinckmann and colleagues' meta-analysis of 46 studies found a positive average association between business planning and small-firm performance and recommended combining planning with learning. Smolka and colleagues' survey of 1,453 entrepreneurs in 25 countries associated the joint use of causal and effectual reasoning with better performance. These findings are not proof that every founder should use the same mixture. [20, 21]

Organizational ambidexterity is the ability to combine exploration and exploitation. Junni and colleagues' meta-analysis found positive performance relationships with substantial contextual and methodological moderation. Davis and colleagues' model of structure in changing markets found costs to both excessive and insufficient structure; the latter could be more damaging within their assumptions. The relevant question is how structure supports adaptation. [22, 50]

There is meaningful evidence against a blanket preference for less planning or greater personality variation. Konradt and colleagues synthesized 33 samples covering 1,885 teams and found a moderate planning-performance association. Peeters and colleagues found higher mean conscientiousness associated with better performance, but greater variation in conscientiousness associated with worse performance. A 2024 systematic narrative review of 47 papers also found positive roles for conscientiousness and agreeableness in collaborative problem solving. [23-25]

These results distinguish diversity of approaches from diversity in willingness or ability to carry out shared commitments. They also leave room for one person, different teams, or different project stages to supply the complementary functions.

## 8. Social psychology: dissent can help when a group can use it

De Dreu and West's two field studies associated minority dissent with innovation when participation in decision making was high. Nemeth and colleagues' experiment found authentic dissent more effective at stimulating solutions than assigned devil's-advocate roles. Together, these findings suggest that a real difference in perspective can matter, and that the group's response to it matters too. [26, 27]

Conflict is not a substitute for useful disagreement. De Wit, Greer and Jehn's meta-analysis of 116 studies and 8,880 groups found consistent negative relationships for relationship and process conflict, with more contingent results for disagreement over the task itself. A person can challenge the prevailing idea while remaining collaborative and dependable. [28]

The social contribution here is opening up deliberation or preventing premature agreement. Neither nonconformity nor being difficult automatically supplies that contribution.

## 9. Collective intelligence: the closest direct experiments

**Small amounts of randomness can break a deadlock.** Shirado and Christakis studied 4,000 people in network-coloring games. Three centrally located bots making occasional random moves improved coordination; the 10% noise condition reduced median solution time by 55.6% relative to all-human controls. Greater noise and different placement did not have the same result. This is causal evidence about a specific intervention in a specific game, not about spontaneous human personalities. [29]

**Independent work can be combined with communication.** Bernstein, Shore and Lazer compared constant, intermittent, and absent social influence in small groups solving route-optimization problems. Intermittent interaction preserved good average performance while retaining high-quality best solutions. A group could therefore obtain complementary benefits by changing when people interact, without sorting people into permanent types. [30]

**Different problem-solving methods can compensate for shared blind spots.** Hong and Page's mathematical model showed conditions under which a functionally diverse group outperformed a group selected for the highest individual scores. Its result depends on task difficulty, the available heuristics, and the process of using others' improvements. It is not a general claim that any kind of diversity outweighs competence. [31]

**Human adaptation can overturn a model's simple recommendation.** Mason and Watts ran 256 experiments with groups of 16. Efficiently connected groups performed better than inefficient networks, contrary to influential simulation results. Humans adapted their exploration rather than merely copying mechanically. This is a reason to test behavioral assumptions before proposing less communication as a universal improvement. [32]

A closer human composition study by Aggarwal and colleagues found an inverted-U association between cognitive-style diversity and collective intelligence across 98 teams. However, those styles concerned information processing, not planning versus spontaneity, and composition was not a randomized personality intervention. [48]

## 10. Cultural evolution and recent research on collective search

Derex and Boyd's innovation experiment showed why different trajectories can be valuable. Initially separated groups could develop alternatives before exchanging information. Seven of twelve partially connected groups reached advanced recombinant innovations, versus none of twelve fully connected groups. The task deliberately made maintaining multiple trajectories useful, so the result supplies a mechanism rather than a universal social prescription. [33]

Deffner and colleagues' 2024 immersive foraging experiment found that heavy use of others' discoveries could benefit individuals while hurting groups in concentrated-resource environments. Group incentives reduced this excessive reliance on others and supported adaptation. This is particularly relevant to a socially useful explorer whose private rewards do not capture the full value of the information generated. [34]

Chirkov and colleagues' September 2026 experiment involved 621 participants tracking a moving resource. Groups outperformed solitary participants when others' payoffs were continuously visible; merely seeing their locations was insufficient and could be harmful. Collective benefit depends on being able to recognize useful information. The study did not show that groups exceeded the best individual in the strictest sense of collective intelligence. [35]

These studies identify an institutional question in addition to a personality question: how can useful discoveries be preserved, evaluated, rewarded, and shared?

## 11. Economics: experimentation produces information others can use

Bolton and Harris's strategic-experimentation model gives the individual-versus-society intuition a formal economic foundation. Trying an uncertain option has an opportunity cost, but what is learned can benefit other people. This information spillover creates incentives to let others do the experimenting and can produce a gap between equilibrium behavior and socially optimal experimentation. The mechanism does not require experimenters to be irrational or personally disorganized. [36]

Azoulay, Graff Zivin and Manso examined research-funding incentives using matched comparisons and difference-in-differences. Scientists supported by a program allowing long horizons, early failures, and changes of direction produced more high-impact research and explored new lines of inquiry relative to comparison scientists. Selection remains a concern because this was not random assignment. The study suggests that permission and support to explore can be consequential, independently of personality. [37]

My inference is that a system may underreward some useful experimenters because other people capture part of their informational contribution. That does not imply every failed experiment created valuable information: learning has to be credible, usable, and worth the costs, including costs imposed on other people.

## 12. Sociology, culture and the production of consequential novelty

Burt's research on structural holes connects good ideas with people who bridge otherwise separated social groups. Such people encounter alternative ways of thinking and can bring them together. This is observational network evidence, but it highlights a different possible source of apparent spontaneity's value: exposure to diverse information, rather than randomness. [38]

Chua, Roth and Lemoine studied global creative crowdsourcing. Cultural tightness was associated with less participation and success in culturally distant creative tasks, yet could help success in familiar cultural contexts. The finding qualifies the idea that permissiveness always helps creativity. Cultural norms are also a different level of analysis from an individual's approach to life planning. [39]

Uzzi and colleagues examined 17.9 million papers and associated high scientific impact with an unusual combination of mostly conventional foundations and some atypical combinations. Wu, Wang and Evans found that smaller teams tended to disrupt existing scientific and technological trajectories while larger teams developed them. Citation-based outcomes are imperfect proxies for social value, and these studies do not measure chaotic personalities. They do support attention to complementary forms of contribution. [40, 41]

## 13. Evolution and development: different strategies can persist

Wolf, van Doorn and Weissing modeled responsive and routine-oriented behavioral types. Responsiveness carried costs and had a larger advantage when rare; experience then reduced its costs. Those mechanisms generated persistent differences. This is a particularly close formal model of coexistence, although responsiveness means sensitivity to information, not random behavior. Evolutionary persistence is not the same as maximizing human wellbeing. [42]

Biological research offers useful analogies. Liang and colleagues found consistent scouting tendencies in honey bees across food and nest-site contexts, with molecular and neurochemical correlates. Rendell and colleagues' social-learning tournament demonstrated how successful copying can be when others' behavior already reflects experience. These are explanations of cooperative search and learning, not evidence for a human lifestyle hierarchy. [43, 44]

Developmental work by Gopnik and colleagues found age-related differences in flexible hypothesis search, including tasks where young children considered possibilities that older participants missed. This offers another alternative to permanent personality specialization: exploration and exploitation may be distributed across life stages. It does not show that children or flexible adults are universally better reasoners. [45]

Nettle's evolutionary account emphasizes trade-offs between fitness costs and benefits. Environmental variation and advantages to rare strategies can preserve differences without any mechanism selecting for a socially optimal population. Group-benefit theories should therefore be compared with these alternatives. [49]

## 14. Philosophy of science and the theories closest to the whole hypothesis

Zollman's models of transient diversity show how a community can benefit by avoiding premature consensus: alternative research programs continue long enough to reveal their merits. However, preserving disagreement indefinitely can also prevent convergence on better-supported conclusions. Wu and O'Connor's 2023 analysis considers how to encourage useful diversity while accounting for time, resources, and ethical constraints. Neither supports indiscriminate contrarianism. [46, 47]

Taylor, Fernandes and Wraight's **complementary cognition** theory is one of the closest articulations of the complete intuition. It proposes that humans cooperate through different, complementary cognitive search specializations. The paper appeared online in 2021 and in a 2022 journal issue. It is an evolutionary synthesis and proposal; the authors explicitly describe substantial work remaining. [3]

Pringle and Robinson's 2024 **diversity advantage** theory likewise proposes group-level functions for personality differences. A 2026 computational extension with Stramer compared different agent compositions across 11 mathematical search landscapes. Mixed compositions often did better; a simple landscape favored a homogeneous high-persistence group. Its mapping from personality to algorithmic behavior is narrow and lacks real-world validation. It is close to the proposed modeling project, but not empirical confirmation about human society. [4, 5]

## 15. What the evidence changes about the original hypothesis

**Supported mechanism:** a group can benefit from activity that looks locally inefficient because it discovers alternatives, breaks a deadlock, or preserves an approach that would otherwise disappear. The clearest examples concern search behavior and interaction structures. [29, 30, 33, 34, 36]

**Unproven link:** people who plan less may or may not do more useful exploration. That relationship must be measured rather than assumed. Likewise, planning can make an execution process more predictable while its eventual social consequences remain surprising. A carefully organized project can create radical change.

**No universal ratio:** favorable mixtures depend on task complexity, volatility, available resources, communication, evaluation quality, and the cost of failure. Stable expertise and novelty seeking can also occur within the same person. A society of adaptive generalists is an important comparison, not merely a society of all planners or all explorers.

**Potential confounds to investigate:** resources may enable experimentation; expertise may enable skilled improvisation; success may permit more autonomy; and survivorship may make successful unconventional people especially visible. These are plausible alternative explanations, not findings proven by this review.

**Define benefit explicitly:** novelty, profits, scientific impact, average group performance, wellbeing, inequality, and resilience are different outcomes. A more variable outcome distribution can increase outstanding successes and costly failures simultaneously. A societal claim must count both and specify who receives the benefits and bears the costs.

## 16. A concrete model and research design for the idea

The question can be modeled productively, but avoid defining one type as the only source of new ideas and another as the only source of implementation. That would largely build complementarity into the assumptions. Give agents independently variable tendencies and skills instead. The design below is a proposal informed by the reviewed literature; no simulation has been run for this report.

**Agent characteristics:** advance planning and investment horizon; directed exploratory search; random choice variability; responsiveness to new evidence; willingness to copy others; persistence; implementation skill; and reliability. Allow correlation between traits, but also test independence and plausible alternative correlations. Represent exploration by what an agent does, rather than by a label assigned in advance.

**Environment:** begin with a problem space containing multiple solutions of different value. Vary whether it is simple or has many misleading local optima, whether the best choices change, and whether useful innovation requires combining discoveries. Include exploration costs, implementation delays, shared information, limited attention, and external costs of failure.

**Critical comparison:** compare fixed specialists, people all using an identical intermediate exploration rate, and adaptive agents who change strategy with circumstances. Keep population size, resources, mean competence, and information access comparable. Also compare mixed personalities with institutional schedules that alternate exploration and execution.

**Outcomes:** measure cumulative net welfare, discovery quality, time to adoption, reliability of essential outputs, adaptation after shocks, individual rewards, inequality, and catastrophic failures. A high best-found solution is not enough if nobody can identify or implement it. Show results across many parameter choices rather than presenting one attractive mixture.

**Predictions worth testing:** additional independent exploration should be more valuable when alternatives are undersearched and knowledge can spread; very rapid imitation may suppress discovery in some settings; excessive switching may prevent implementation; and costly learning may justify specialist roles. Environmental change can increase exploration's value, but if change is too fast for information to remain useful, it can also reduce the returns to learning.

**Human validation:** measure openness, planning habits, conscientiousness facets, and behavioral exploration separately; form groups with equal mean skill but different compositions; manipulate task uncertainty and incentives; and measure novelty, usefulness, completion, and costs. For social relevance, follow naturally occurring teams or communities over time, including unsuccessful efforts, rather than selecting only famous successful improvisers.

The most valuable unresolved question is: **When does persistent variation between people create benefits that cannot be reproduced by giving everyone good opportunities and incentives to switch intelligently between exploration and execution?** Answering that would move the original intuition from a plausible synthesis toward a distinct empirical contribution.

## 17. Suggested reading route

Start with **March [1]** for the basic tradeoff and **Smaldino et al. [2]** for the modern interdisciplinary synthesis. Read **Shirado and Christakis [29]** for the cleanest demonstration involving randomness, and **Bolton and Harris [36]** for the gap between private experimentation incentives and shared benefits.

For the life-path question, use **Mitchell et al. [14]**, **Cheng et al. [10]**, and **Riddell et al. [11]**. For a fair test of the hypothesis, pair them with the planning and team-composition evidence in **Konradt et al. [23]** and **Peeters et al. [24]**.

For theories closest to the complete idea, read **Taylor et al. [3]** and **Pringle et al. [5]**, retaining their theoretical status. The search terms most likely to connect these fields are: *collective exploration-exploitation; transient diversity; complementary cognition; strategic experimentation; cognitive division of labor; planned happenstance; effectuation; and adaptive flexibility.*

[Meta-analytic relations between personality and cognitive ability](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10266031/). *PNAS, 120, e2212794120*.

**Evidence:** Meta-analytic synthesis. Distinguishes broad traits, aspects and facets. Industriousness relates positively to originality/creativity ability, illustrating the limits of an order-versus-creativity dichotomy.

**Evidence:** Human experiment. Separates information-seeking and choice variability in 30 participants. Evidence about how exploration is deployed; random noise was not optimal in this task's normative model.

### [10] Cheng, C., Lau, H.-P. B., & Chan, M.-P. S. (2014)

[Coping flexibility and psychological adjustment to stressful life changes: A meta-analytic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25222637/). *Psychological Bulletin, 140, 1582-1607*.

**Evidence:** Meta-analysis. 58,946 participants; situation-sensitive adaptation shows stronger associations than variability alone. Individual adjustment, not social innovation, is the outcome.

### [11] Riddell, H., et al. (2026; online 2025)

[A meta-analytic review and conceptual model of the antecedents and outcomes of goal adjustment in response to striving difficulties](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02312-4). *Nature Human Behaviour, 10, 317-332*.

**Evidence:** Meta-analysis. 235 studies; distinguishes disengagement, reengagement and flexibility. Authors rate accumulated evidence low to moderate because of design, heterogeneity and publication-bias limitations.

### [12] Aeon, B., Faber, A., & Panaccio, A. (2021)

[Does time management work? A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0245066). *PLOS ONE, 16, e0245066*.

**Evidence:** Meta-analysis. 158 studies; positive relationships with performance and wellbeing. Time management includes adaptation, and much of the evidence is correlational.

### [13] Moffitt, T. E., et al. (2011)

[A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21262822/). *PNAS, 108, 2693-2698*.

**Evidence:** Longitudinal observational study. Birth-cohort and sibling evidence links self-control with later life outcomes. Useful counterweight to conflating exploration with poor regulation; causality is not experimentally established.

### [14] Mitchell, K. E., Levin, A. S., & Krumboltz, J. D. (1999)

[Planned Happenstance: Constructing Unexpected Career Opportunities](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1556-6676.1999.tb02431.x). *Journal of Counseling & Development, 77, 115-124*.

**Evidence:** Career theory. Frames chance events as opportunities that active exploration can help create and use. Conceptual foundation, not a comparative trial of planned versus unplanned lives.

### [15] Kim, B., et al. (2018; online 2016)

[Longitudinal Relationships Between Planned Happenstance Skills and Life Adjustment and the Moderating Role of Career Barriers](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894845316681078). *Journal of Career Development, 45(3)*.

**Evidence:** Longitudinal observational study. 307 Korean students during school-to-work transition. A bundled set of happenstance skills preceded adjustment; contextual and causal limits remain.

### [16] Pryor, R. G. L., & Bright, J. (2003)

[The Chaos Theory of Careers](https://doi.org/10.1177/103841620301200304). *Australian Journal of Career Development, 12(3), 12-20*.

**Evidence:** Career theory. Emphasizes complexity, chance and nonlinear changes in careers. The term chaos describes career dynamics, rather than a validated personality category.

### [17] Tonietto, G. N., & Malkoc, S. A. (2016)

[The Calendar Mindset: Scheduling Takes the Fun Out and Puts the Work In](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.14.0591). *Journal of Marketing Research, 53(6)*.

**Evidence:** Series of experiments. 13 studies identify costs of precise scheduling for leisure enjoyment, removed by rough scheduling. Directly relevant to everyday spontaneity but narrow in scope.

### [18] Sio, U. N., & Ormerod, T. C. (2009)
